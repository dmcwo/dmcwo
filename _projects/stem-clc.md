The Learning Assistants were holding study sessions outside on benches and in overcrowded dining halls. The location changed week to week, which meant students who wanted to come sometimes couldn't find them. The program was growing fast and it had nowhere to go.

That conversation with CEILS staff started the STEM Collaborative Learning Center.

UCLA's Center for Education Innovation and Learning in the Sciences (CEILS) ran a Learning Assistant (LA) program pairing trained undergraduate LAs with large introductory science, engineering, and math courses. Pilot studies had shown that attendance at LA-supported sessions correlated with significantly higher grades. The program was expanding and it needed a dedicated home. Library staff, hearing about the challenge, saw the opportunity: the Science and Engineering Library's Research Commons was heavily used in some hours and underutilized in others. A proposal began to take shape.

The proposal was straightforward in principle and harder to move in practice. The Research Commons was a shared space with existing users and existing claims. Some library stakeholders heard "giving away space" where the proposal actually said "maximizing it." The early conversations involved a lot of words like "ceding" and "losing." Getting past that required reframing the question: not "whose space is this?" but "what should this space be for?" When the answer became "supporting student success and campus partnership," the conversation shifted.

The project team — library staff, CEILS, and UCLA's Program for Excellence in Education and Research in the Sciences (PEERS) — agreed to repurpose an existing space with minimal budget impact, using furniture already on hand. The total materials cost was $785.76: four stanchions, two mobile whiteboards, and two poster sign holders to define the space and support the new configuration. Research on active learning environments supported the low-tech approach: studies in the field had shown that flexible seating and writable surfaces mattered more to student success than expensive technology. We planned the space accordingly, using usage headcount data to determine how much of the room could be dedicated to LA sessions while still protecting open study space for students who needed it.

The STEM CLC opened in September 2017.

Within the first year, the results exceeded what the original proposal had promised. The number of Learning Assistants increased from 90 per quarter to 238; instructor participation more than doubled, from 7–10 to 20–25 courses per quarter. More than 20% of all students served through the LA program attended sessions in the STEM CLC. In Fall 2017 alone, more than 5,000 students had access to collaborative office hours outside of class time. The Science and Engineering Library's overall space usage increased 17%, from 68,199 visits in 2016–17 to 79,524 in 2017–18. Over the project's duration, students logged 19,510+ hours of peer-to-peer collaborative learning in the space. Eighty-six percent of students who attended workshops and collaborative office hours agreed or strongly agreed that those interactions helped them learn the material.

The STEM CLC also prompted change beyond the space itself. The Sciences Libraries developed a new Learning Spaces Policy dedicating half of their classroom space to campus groups focused on scholarship and research, with special consideration for learning communities that lacked official space support — like the LA program and PEERS. The CLC's success attracted donor interest in renovating the entire Science and Engineering Library. It also modeled a service approach that spread: inspired by the CLC, other campus departments began offering collaborative office hours in their own spaces, and the LA program more than doubled the total students it reached — from 10,818 to 24,874 — as that expansion took hold. Campus-wide exposure to collaborative learning increased by 30–50%.

The space kept growing. In Winter 2019, the UCLA Undergraduate Writing Center added a dedicated service point in the STEM CLC — the first on the south side of campus near the STEM departments — with a 92% appointment fill rate in its first two quarters. In Winter 2020, the Library added an embedded research assistance service point staffed by graduate students from the Information Studies program.

The project gave me a close look at what it takes to make institutional change from inside a library. The internal stakeholder work — rewriting policy, reframing purpose, building the case incrementally — mattered as much as the space design itself. And partnering with CEILS and PEERS meant co-designing around programs that were already working and whose data on equity and student achievement made the library's value concrete in a way that gate counts and LibGuide views rarely can. This was a case where focusing on a shared mission turned out to be the most practical strategy available.
